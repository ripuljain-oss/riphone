Jeremy Hsu, reporting for [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/06/sp-500-blocks-fast-spacex-entry-wont-waive-rule-for-unprofitable-ai-firms/):

> But in its final decision, the S&P Dow Jones Indices stated that “no changes will be made to the eligibility criteria including financial viability screens, seasoning period, or minimum IWF.” Even after the standard yearlong wait, SpaceX, Anthropic, and OpenAI may struggle to deliver the consistent profitability necessary to qualify for the S&P 500.

The funny thing about a trillion-dollar IPO is that the public-market debut is not really the endgame. The index is.

SpaceX reportedly wanted unusually fast access to the S&P 500, despite offering only about three percent of its IPO shares to public investors and carrying a $29 billion debt load tied to its AI infrastructure buildout. That is the part that makes the request so revealing. This was not just about a ticker symbol, liquidity, or Wall Street ceremony. It was about getting passive money forced into the trade.

Ars cites Bloomberg Intelligence estimates that S&P 500 inclusion would have triggered $14 billion of passive fund buying for SpaceX. OpenAI and Anthropic could have picked up $8 billion and $4.6 billion from similar treatment. That is not demand in the normal sense. That is index mechanics. Vanguard, Fidelity, retirement plans, target-date funds, all buying because the benchmark says so.

There is a world where that is fine. The S&P 500 is full of companies ordinary investors would never underwrite individually, but they own anyway through the index. The bargain is that the index has rules: seasoning, float, profitability. Boring screens that keep it from becoming a growth-stock launchpad with better distribution.

What SpaceX asked for was the distribution without the boredom.

That is why S&P Dow Jones Indices saying no matters. Not because index committees are heroic guardians of capitalism. They are not. But because one of the few useful frictions left in the market is the refusal to convert every sufficiently hyped private company into an automatic allocation for people who think they are just buying “the market.”

Nasdaq and FTSE Russell moved faster. The S&P 500 did not. For once, the slower institution had the cleaner product instinct.
