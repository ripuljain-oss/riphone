Space Daily, on the AI doing actual work while we argue about chatbots:

Most AI coverage is a loop now. Something ships, someone claims it changes everything, someone else calls it hype, both are partially right, repeat. I have developed a reflex for it. Then I read this and the reflex failed.

The Herculaneum scrolls were buried under volcanic ash when Vesuvius erupted in 79 AD. They were carbonized. Unreadable. For centuries the standard approach was to unroll them physically, which destroyed most of what survived. Now a machine-learning model scans X-rays of the charred papyrus and detects faint carbon ink traces that no human eye can resolve. The first word recovered this way was the Greek word for purple, in October 2023. By February 2024, they had over 2,000 characters from a philosophical text that has been underground for two millennia.

> The model flags where ink is likely to be, and papyrologists confirm the letters and the sense.

That sentence is the whole story. The algorithm doesn't read the scroll. It returns a probability. The papyrologist looks at what it flagged and decides. This is not AI replacing expertise. It is AI doing the one thing that was previously impossible: getting through the haystack.

The same pattern runs through every example in the piece. The Euclid mission handed an algorithm a million galaxy images and received back 497 strong gravitational lens candidates. A separate Hubble project surfaced 1,400 anomalous objects from 99.6 million images. AlphaFold predicted the folded structure of 200 million proteins and won a Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2024. Not a product award. A Nobel.

None of these systems understand what they are looking at. They are filters operating at scales no human team could cover in a lifetime. The human still confirms the discovery. The human still decides what it means. The algorithm does the previously impossible part.

This is worth sitting with the next time someone's chatbot demo fails to impress you. The version of AI that matters most right now is not trying to sound smart. It is reading things that have been unreadable for two thousand years.
